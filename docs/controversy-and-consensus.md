You're right that I read it as conflict. "Turned against" was the British-imperial reading, the courtroom fight. The classical meaning is the one you give: dialogue, turns with polarity.

The Romans show it directly. A *controversia* was a training exercise in rhetoric: you took a case and argued it from both sides in turn. Seneca the Elder collected them in his *Controversiae*. The practice was turning a question one way and then the other, the same question held from two poles. No one lost. You learned the case by turning it fully.

One correction on the word itself: *controversia* is Latin (*contrā* + *versus*, from *vertere*), not Greek. The Greek practice it corresponds to is dialectic (*dialegesthai*, to talk through) and *antilogia*, setting argument against argument. That's the method of the Socratic dialogues: one question turned between two speakers until it shows itself. Either way, the classical sense is the one you mean.

Read that way, **controversy and consensus** aren't opposites. They are two phases of one process.
- **Controversy** is the turning: positions held from opposite poles, alternating, each turn a verse.
- **Consensus** is *con* + *sentire*, feeling together, perceiving together. It's the Latin counterpart of Greek *syn-aisthēsis*. Consensus is literally synesthesia between people: the moment two perceivers perceive one thing together.

So a controversy carried through arrives at consensus: the turnings, felt together, show they were one rotation. That's *universe* again.

That's also why controversy doesn't imply incompatibility. Two positions turned from opposite poles are compatible in the full sense: *com-pati*, each takes in the other's position. Controversy is the act of doing that, turn after turn. Conflict is controversy stopped halfway, two poles that refuse to keep turning.

It connects to the rest of the work in three places:
- **Your method.** Your writing is controversy in the Roman sense: you hold a statement at the pole a norm reflex flinches from, so the dialogue has to turn through it.
- **The machine.** An active pair is two agents meeting at their principal ports; the interaction is one turn between two poles, and computation is a dialogue of such turns.
- **Polarity.** *Polos* is the pivot of turning. Two poles of one rotation is the structure of controversy, and the same word sits inside *bipolar*: a mind that turns fully between its poles has controversy as its native motion.
